1The Paragon Tier: earning your place in New Zealand's economy

A government-recognised top tier of New Zealand businesses that companies must compete to enter, and can be removed from.

1.1Background to the issue

New Zealand's economy is growing again, but only at around 1–2% in 2026, and almost all of that comes from lower interest rates and migration rather than businesses actually getting better at what they do. Politicians need to address this because a country cannot keep raising living standards by simply adding more people — each worker has to produce more value.

Citizens should care because productivity is what pays for wages, hospitals and schools. When productivity stalls, wages stall with it, and every New Zealander feels it in their pay packet and in the quality of public services they receive.

1.2Statement of the problem

The specific problem is that New Zealand businesses have no real reason to improve. Once a company receives a government grant, subsidy or contract, it usually keeps it regardless of how well it performs afterwards. This has been caused by decades of support schemes that are handed out but almost never reviewed, and by cheap borrowing after the OCR fell from 5.5% to 2.25%, which flows into property rather than productive business.

The evidence that Aotearoa needs change is clear: the OECD's 2026 survey of New Zealand explicitly warned that our productivity growth is weak and that migration is not a substitute for it. This affects all of New Zealand, but hits workers hardest — if firms don't produce more value per worker, employers have no room to raise wages, and young people entering the workforce inherit an economy that has stopped moving forward.

1.3What the current government does

The current government's main economic tool is the Investment Boost tax break, introduced by the National-led coalition, which gives businesses a deduction for investment spending. The problem is that it rewards any spending at all, with no test afterwards to check whether the business actually became more productive — a firm can claim it and change nothing.

The Reserve Bank has also cut the OCR to 2.25%, but monetary policy is a short-term cyclical tool and cannot fix a structural problem; historically that cheap credit has gone into housing rather than into exporting businesses. The OECD has recommended structural reforms such as easing foreign investment rules and strengthening competition, but these remain recommendations only, with no law, deadline or accountability attached.

Across all of these, the same flaw repeats: support is given out, and nobody ever checks what happened next.

1.4Our policy

The Paragon Alliance will create the Paragon Tier — a government-recognised top tier of New Zealand businesses that companies must compete to enter and can be removed from. Companies will be scored by an independent body on four measures:

  • ProductivityValue produced per worker, measured year on year.
  • Export growthEarnings brought in from outside New Zealand.
  • Wages paidWhat the firm actually pays its staff.
  • Staff trainingSkills invested in the existing workforce.

Illustrative only. These companies are invented for this project and the numbers are made up to show how the mechanism would behave — a bank losing its place, a five-person software firm earning one, and a firm that fell out climbing back in.

Those who make the Tier receive real advantages — priority access to government contracts, faster consenting and permits, and a reduced company tax rate for as long as they hold their place. Crucially, membership is not permanent: every company is re-scored every two years, and any firm whose numbers fall is removed, no matter how large or long-established it is. All scores are published publicly, so customers, investors and workers can see exactly who is performing. Any business can apply at any time, and the entry test is identical for a five-person startup and a major bank.

This solves the problem better than current policy because it attaches a consequence to failure, which no existing scheme does. The benefits are that government support finally flows to firms that deliver, businesses gain a permanent reason to keep improving, and workers get public information about which employers are worth working for. Society would begin to see change within two to three years, when the first re-scoring round removes underperforming firms and the public list starts to shift; the productivity and wage effects would follow over roughly five to ten years.

1.5Obstacles, and how we handle them

There are real obstacles, and we would rather name them than pretend they don't exist.

The obstacle

Large established companies will resist a system that can strip their status.

Our response

The scoring body is fully independent and legally protected from political pressure, so size and lobbying cannot buy a place.

The obstacle

Smaller businesses may argue the scoring favours firms who can afford good reporting.

Our response

Free reporting support for small businesses, so the test measures performance rather than paperwork.

The obstacle

If we choose the wrong measures, companies will game the numbers instead of actually improving.

Our response

Scoring criteria are published openly and reviewed after the first cycle, so gaming can be identified and closed off.

1.6Conclusion

New Zealand's economy is not short of money — it is short of reasons to improve. Every current policy hands out support and then stops paying attention. The Paragon Tier changes that by making excellence something you have to earn again every two years, with public scores, real rewards, and real consequences.

If you believe that support should follow performance rather than reputation, and that your wages should rise because your employer is actually getting better, then vote for the Paragon Alliance. We are not asking you to trust businesses — we are asking you to make them prove it.

2Sponsored Housing Districts: building homes New Zealanders can actually afford

Companies fund and build whole districts, including the infrastructure. Affordability is written into a binding contract, and it is reviewed every three years.

2.1Background to the issue

Housing in New Zealand has been unaffordable relative to income for well over a decade, and the situation is not improving fast enough. Politicians must address this because housing is the single largest cost most households face, and it sits underneath almost every other social problem — health, education and poverty all worsen when families cannot secure a stable home.

Citizens should be concerned because the social housing waitlist has exceeded 20,000 applicants while the government's own housing agency delivers only around 1,500 new builds a year. At that rate, the gap does not close — it widens every single year.

2.2Statement of the problem

The specific problem is that not enough houses are being built, and the ones that are built are not affordable. This has been caused by two things working together: the government has relied on one agency, Kāinga Ora, which has proven it cannot build at the scale required, and private developers have no commercial reason to build affordable housing because it is simply less profitable than building for the top of the market.

The evidence is stark — Kāinga Ora's debt was forecast to reach $24.8 billion by 2026/27, an independent review found the agency financially unviable without major change, and the waitlist continues to sit above 20,000. This affects renters, families in emergency and motel accommodation, and young New Zealanders who cannot buy a first home. The consequence is a country where wealth stays concentrated in the generation that already owns property, and where the next generation rents indefinitely.

2.3What the current government does

The National-led coalition's Kāinga Ora Turnaround Plan focuses on reducing the agency's debt and bringing build costs down to market rate. The issue is that it is a cost-control plan, not a building plan — its target output remains around 1,500 new builds a year, nowhere near the scale of the waitlist.

The coalition's Going for Housing Growth programme frees up land and improves infrastructure financing, and the enabling legislation passed in July 2026, but councils will not receive their first incentive payments until April 2027, meaning years will pass before it produces a single completed home.

Budget 2026 also raised the Minimum Income-Related Rent contribution from 25% to 30% of income for social housing tenants. That change may make the support system fairer between renters, but it does not add one house — it simply shifts more of the cost onto the people least able to carry it.

2.4Our policy

The Paragon Alliance will introduce Sponsored Housing Districts. Under this policy, companies will be able to fund and build entire residential districts, including the infrastructure that goes with them — roads, water, and power connections — rather than leaving councils and Kāinga Ora to carry that cost alone. In return, sponsors receive naming rights over the district and a reduced company tax rate for as long as they maintain it to standard.

In exchange, a fixed share of every sponsored district must be sold or rented at below-market rates, and this is written directly into a binding sponsorship agreement rather than left to goodwill. Every sponsorship is reviewed every three years: if maintenance slips or the affordable share is not honoured, the tax reduction ends and the naming rights are stripped. Alongside this, Kāinga Ora will stop trying to be a construction company and become a tenancy manager, focusing on supporting tenants — the work it is genuinely good at.

This solves the problem better than current policy because it brings in private capital at a scale no single Crown agency can match, while locking affordability into a contract instead of hoping the market delivers it. The benefits are more homes built per year, infrastructure funded upfront rather than stalling councils, and a legally enforceable affordability guarantee. Society would begin to see the first completed sponsored districts within three to four years, given consenting and construction time, with a measurable effect on the waitlist and on price-to-income ratios by around year five.

2.5Obstacles, and how we handle them

We are honest about the downsides.

The obstacle

Many New Zealanders will be uncomfortable with companies naming suburbs.

Our response

Naming is capped at a single discreet marker. No corporate branding throughout a district.

The obstacle

A real risk of creating visibly "branded" low-income neighbourhoods.

Our response

Affordable homes must be distributed evenly across each development, never clustered in one section.

The obstacle

A sponsor could go bankrupt part-way through a build.

Our response

Sponsors must hold a completion bond so the Crown can finish any abandoned project.

The obstacle

Councils may resist local planning being shaped by whoever is paying.

Our response

Councils get a binding veto over which sites are eligible for sponsorship in the first place.

2.6Conclusion

New Zealand does not have a housing plan — it has a housing agency that cannot keep up and a set of reforms that will not reach families for years. Sponsored Housing Districts bring in the capital, the speed and the scale that one government agency cannot provide, and it locks affordability into a contract with real consequences for anyone who breaks it.

We would rather be honest about the trade-offs than promise you a solution that arrives in 2030. If you want homes built in years rather than decades, and you want affordability guaranteed in writing rather than hoped for, vote for the Paragon Alliance. We build, or we lose the contract — that is the standard we hold ourselves to as well.

3Statement of intent: our vision, and who we would work with

What we are trying to build, and the one party we would sit down with first.

3.1Our vision statement

We want a New Zealand where public help has to be earned, and then earned again. Every grant, tax break and contract should come with a clear test, a public score, and a real chance of losing it. We want the best firms to rise because they are the best, and families to find a home that is affordable because someone signed a contract saying so.

3.2Coalition statement of intent

If we win seats, our first coalition offer goes to The Opportunity Party.

We choose them because we agree on what is broken. We say cheap money keeps flowing into land and houses instead of into firms that make things and pay wages. Their 2026 Tax Reset says the same thing and acts on it: tax land, not work.

  • $19,400
    The tax-free Citizen's Income in their Tax Reset, paid for by a land value tax. Opportunity Party, Tax Reset 2026

That fits our two policies. The Paragon Tier rewards firms that lift output and wages; their reset stops the tax system quietly pulling money the other way. Sponsored Housing Districts add homes; a land tax makes sitting on empty land expensive, so more of it gets built on.

We do not agree on everything. They would tax land harder than we would, and we would cut company tax for firms that earn their place. We will not pretend that gap away. But a coalition needs a shared diagnosis more than a shared wish list — and on the diagnosis, we are already together.
